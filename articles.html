Understanding Hoarding

Understanding Hoarding Disorder: A Guide for Houston Families

Hoarding disorder is classified in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as a distinct condition. It is characterized by persistent difficulty discarding possessions, regardless of their actual value. This difficulty is driven by a perceived need to save items and by distress associated with letting them go.

For families in Houston, understanding that hoarding is a mental health condition — not laziness, stubbornness, or a character flaw — is the essential first step toward helping a loved one. An estimated 2–6% of the population is affected by hoarding disorder, which means tens of thousands of Houston-area residents may be living in hoarding conditions right now.

The causes of hoarding are complex. Research points to a combination of genetic factors, brain chemistry, traumatic life events, and co-occurring conditions like depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD. Hoarding often begins or worsens after a significant life event — the death of a loved one, a divorce, job loss, or a health crisis.

In Houston specifically, hurricanes and flooding events like Hurricane Harvey (2017) have been linked to increased hoarding behavior. The trauma of losing possessions in a flood can trigger an intense desire to accumulate and hold onto items as a coping mechanism.

How to Help a Loved One

If you are concerned about a family member's hoarding behavior, the most important thing is to approach the conversation with empathy, not criticism. Avoid ultimatums like "clean this up or else." Instead, express concern for their health and safety. Offer to help, and let them know professional services exist that are compassionate and confidential.

Professional hoarding cleanup works best when combined with ongoing mental health support. A therapist specializing in hoarding disorder can help the individual address the underlying causes while the cleanup team addresses the physical environment. This combination produces the best long-term results and reduces the likelihood of recurrence.

Key Statistics

  • 2–6% of the population has hoarding disorder
  • 50% of people with hoarding also have depression
  • Hoarding typically begins by age 15–19 but worsens with age
  • 75% of people with hoarding have a co-occurring anxiety disorder
  • Hoarding is 3x more common in adults over 55

Houston-Specific

Mold, Heat & Humidity: Why Houston Hoarding Situations Are Uniquely Dangerous

Houston's subtropical climate creates conditions that make hoarding situations more dangerous than in most other U.S. cities. Understanding these local factors helps families recognize the urgency of seeking professional help.

The Mold Factor

Houston averages 75–80% relative humidity throughout the year. In a hoarded home where items are stacked against walls, blocking air circulation and HVAC vents, humidity levels can exceed 90% in localized areas. Mold can begin growing on organic materials — paper, cardboard, fabric, wood — within 24–48 hours at these humidity levels.

Black mold (Stachybotrys chartarum) is particularly concerning because it produces mycotoxins that cause respiratory problems, headaches, chronic fatigue, and in severe cases, neurological symptoms. In a hoarded home, mold can grow behind stacked items for months or years before anyone notices.

Extreme Heat Risks

Houston summers regularly bring temperatures above 95°F with heat indices exceeding 110°F. In a hoarded home, piled items absorb and radiate heat, while blocked HVAC systems cannot cool the space effectively. This creates heat stroke risk for residents, especially elderly individuals. The heat also accelerates decomposition of organic materials and intensifies odors.

Pest Pressures

Houston's warm, humid climate supports year-round pest activity. American cockroaches (the large "water bugs" common in Houston), German cockroaches, rodents, fire ants, termites, and even snakes can establish populations in hoarded homes. These pests carry disease, contaminate food and surfaces, and cause structural damage.

Flood Vulnerability

Houston's flat terrain and extensive impervious surface coverage make it one of the most flood-prone cities in America. Hoarded homes that flood face catastrophic consequences — contaminated floodwater wicks up through paper, fabric, and organic materials, creating biohazard conditions that can render a home uninhabitable. Post-Harvey, we worked with numerous families whose pre-existing hoarding situations were dramatically worsened by flood damage.

Practical Guide

How to Choose a Hoarding Cleanup Company in Houston

Choosing the right hoarding cleanup company is critical. The wrong choice can result in traumatic experiences for the homeowner, incomplete cleanup, or exposure to health hazards. Here is what to look for when evaluating Houston-area providers.

Compassion and Training

The most important quality in a hoarding cleanup company is not their equipment — it is their approach to the people they serve. Ask about their team's training in hoarding disorder awareness and compassionate communication. A company that treats hoarding as "just a mess to clean up" will likely cause emotional harm to the homeowner and deliver poor results.

Insurance and Licensing

Verify that the company carries general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage. In Texas, there is no specific "hoarding cleanup license," but reputable companies maintain proper business registration and insurance. If biohazard services are offered, ask about their compliance with OSHA regulations and Texas DSHS waste disposal requirements.

Transparent Pricing

A reputable company provides a free on-site estimate with a clear, written breakdown of costs. Be wary of companies that quote sight-unseen or refuse to put pricing in writing. Ask about their payment structure — most legitimate companies do not require large upfront deposits.

Confidentiality Practices

Ask specifically about their confidentiality protocols. Will they arrive in unmarked vehicles? Do they have a policy against discussing client situations? Will they sign a confidentiality agreement if requested? Privacy matters deeply in hoarding situations.

Red Flags to Watch For

Be cautious of companies that: provide quotes without visiting the property, pressure you to sign immediately, lack insurance documentation, use judgmental language about hoarding, or refuse to involve the homeowner in sorting decisions. These are signs of a company that prioritizes speed over compassion and thoroughness.

For Families

Helping an Elderly Parent with Hoarding: A Step-by-Step Guide

One of the most common calls we receive comes from adult children who are concerned about an aging parent's hoarding behavior. This guide provides practical steps for approaching the situation with care.

Start with a Conversation, Not an Intervention

Before calling any cleanup service, have an honest conversation with your parent. Express concern for their safety and health — not frustration with the mess. Use "I" statements: "I worry about you falling" rather than "You need to clean this up." Listen to their perspective. They may not see the situation the same way you do, and that is normal with hoarding disorder.

Involve a Mental Health Professional

If your parent is open to it, connecting with a therapist who specializes in hoarding before cleanup begins dramatically improves outcomes. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has shown the best results for hoarding disorder. Several Houston-area therapists specialize in this area.

Focus on Safety First

If the situation is dangerous — blocked exits, trip hazards, pest infestation, mold exposure — safety takes priority. You may need to involve Adult Protective Services (APS) if your parent is at immediate risk and unwilling to accept help. In Texas, you can report concerns by calling the APS hotline at 1-800-458-9858.

Choose the Right Cleanup Partner

Select a cleanup company that your parent feels comfortable with. This may mean having the company's representative meet with your parent before any work begins. A good company will take the time to build trust and answer questions. The cleanup process should happen at a pace your parent can handle emotionally.

Plan for Ongoing Support

Cleanup is not a one-time fix. Without ongoing support — therapy, regular check-ins, possibly a professional organizer — hoarding behavior often returns within 1–2 years. Develop a long-term plan that includes continued mental health support and regular home visits.

After the Cleanup

Preventing Hoarding Relapse: Maintaining a Clean Home After Cleanup

A successful cleanup is a major achievement, but it is just the beginning of the journey. Research shows that without ongoing support, many individuals return to hoarding behavior within 12–18 months. Here are strategies that help maintain a clean, safe home after professional cleanup.

Continue Therapy

Ongoing cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the single most effective tool for preventing hoarding relapse. A therapist can help address the underlying anxiety and attachment patterns that drive accumulation. Many Houston therapists offer telehealth sessions, making it easier to maintain regular appointments.

Establish Routines

Simple daily and weekly routines prevent gradual accumulation. A "one in, one out" rule — for every new item brought in, one item leaves — helps maintain balance. Setting a daily 15-minute decluttering habit can prevent small piles from becoming big ones.

Build a Support Network

Support groups, both in-person and online, connect individuals with others who understand hoarding disorder. The International OCD Foundation maintains a directory of hoarding support groups. Having someone to call when the urge to accumulate feels overwhelming can make the difference between maintaining progress and relapsing.

Schedule Regular Check-Ins

Whether it is a family member, friend, or professional organizer, having someone visit the home regularly creates accountability and catches early signs of re-accumulation before they become overwhelming. Many of our clients schedule periodic check-in visits with our team to help maintain their progress.
